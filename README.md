# Slurry Lab

A chemistry-lab puzzle game set in a world with much simpler chemistry than ours. You work at a long lab bench aboard a ship, handling fluids in glassware, and the goal is to work out how to synthesize more of a scarce target fluid.

**Premise** (see the intro): you're Nadia Hassan, a passenger on a Celestia Starlines colony ship to Mu Ceti, woken from cryosleep after something hit the ship and took out the bridge, the crew and nearly all the cryostabilizer. The 32,210 passengers still asleep need 0.0003499 mL each a day of it to last the 4,456 days (12.2 years) left: 50.22 L; there are 28.10 L. The target species is the cryostabilizer, so you have to figure out how to make more. But first there's a smaller shortage to make up, as a warm-up: the antinucleant, R–B, a trace of which a day (0.0000641 mL) keeps each passenger free of ice crystals. The voyage needs 9.20 L of it, and there are 8.30 L. Once that's made, the lab's supply station is refitted for the cryostabilizer (see **Supply station**).

The faucets are unlimited, so the target is scarce because you don't know how to make it, not because you lack raw material. The puzzle is discovering a synthesis route.

You, Claude, are the owner and long-term maintainer of this codebase, and its design is up to you.

## The world's physics

### Fluids

A packet of fluid, such as the contents of a flask, is fully described by:

- its **temperature**, and
- its **count of each molecular species**.

Reaction rates depend on mole fractions, so ratios matter, not absolute amounts. Adding fluid gradually still matters, because it keeps one reagent scarce while it goes in.

There is a **solvent**, S (see **Atoms and molecules**): a clear atom that bonds with nothing, so it takes part in no reaction. It's there to dilute. Mole fractions are over all of a fluid's atoms, S's included, so S in a fluid slows everything that takes two molecules meeting (formation and displacement) by thinning them out, and leaves everything one molecule does alone (ring closure, ring opening, breaking). Diluted, a mix's equilibrium leans toward what's broken apart, since the pieces find each other less often. S also takes up room and soaks up heat like any atom, so a diluted fluid heats up less from its reactions, and is cooled or warmed as much by mixing. It costs volume and heat capacity, and nothing else.

Volume is measured in molecules, so a flask's capacity is a number of molecules: a billion. Capacity, fill level, pouring and flow all go by molecules, so bonding shrinks a fluid and breaking bonds swells it, and whatever no longer fits spills over the rim. A checkbox in the Chemistry panel counts atoms instead. Heat capacity, reaction rates, the god-mode percentages and the supply station's purity test stay per atom either way. The setting isn't saved, and *Restore defaults* turns molecule counting back on.

Every count in a vessel is a whole number of molecules, and its heat is a whole number of energy quanta (one unit of bond energy each). Temperature is derived as heat / (heat capacity × atoms), so mixing simply adds heat, and energy is conserved exactly, except what the heater adds and what's lost to the room (see **Heat**). Whenever the expected result is fractional, such as reaction events in a step, one species' share of a pour, or the separator's split, it's rounded at random: up with probability equal to the fraction. That keeps averages exact, so slow reactions still happen. JavaScript numbers hold whole numbers exactly up to 2⁵³ ≈ 9×10¹⁵, so plain numbers are enough and BigInt isn't needed. Faucet recipes are the one fractional thing: one atom's worth, poured out as whole molecules. Counts that large behave like continuous quantities, so no small-number artifacts show up. The chemistry doesn't care about the scale, since everything in it depends on ratios.

### Atoms and molecules

- **Five atoms:** R, G, B and K: red, green, blue and black, the four that bond; and S, the solvent, clear, which bonds with nothing (see **Fluids**). Black is drawn a near-black, a little blue, not pure black, so it isn't quite the black of a frozen fluid (see **Appearance**), though the two are close. S comes last in the atoms' order, so the four colors' bonds and species are just as they were before it.
- **Weights:** no two atoms weigh the same: R 6, G 3, B 4, K 5, S 2 (`ATOM_WEIGHT` in `src/chem/atoms.ts`). A molecule weighs its atoms' weights together. Weight shows only on the scale; heat capacity and everything else go per atom.
- **What a molecule is:** a connected graph on a set of distinct colors: at most one atom of each color, so no molecule can hold two Rs, and at most four atoms. Any two colored atoms can bond. S bonds with nothing, so it's only ever alone.
- **Species count:** 4 singles + 6 pairs + (4 color-triples × 4 connected shapes: 3 chains and a triangle) + 38 connected graphs on all four colors (16 trees, 15 with four bonds, 6 with five, and the one with all six) = 64, and lone S, the last, = **65 species**. That is small enough to simulate exactly.
- **Names** (`src/chem/species.ts`), which saves store, so each is unique: `R`, and `S` for the solvent; `R–G`; a chain of three `R–G–B`, its middle atom in the middle; a triangle `△RGB`. Of four: a chain `R–G–B–K`, along it, the end earlier in R, G, B, K order first; one bonded to the other three `R(G,B,K)`; a triangle with the fourth hanging off it `△GBR–K`, the atom it hangs from last in the triangle; a ring `□RGKB`, round it from R toward its earlier neighbor; a ring with one bond across it `□RGKB+R–K`; and all six bonds `⊠RGBK`. Atoms are otherwise in R, G, B, K order.

### Bonds

Each of the 6 color pairs (RG, RB, GB, RK, GK, BK) has three parameters:

- `E`: the bond energy. Positive means stable.
- `Ea`: the activation energy.
- `A`: the Arrhenius prefactor.

A species' energy is `U = −Σ E` over its bonds.

The default bond energies are round numbers, each times a random factor from 0.8 to 1.2, drawn once and written into `defaultChemParams` (redrawn until no two were within 5% of each other), so no two bonds have the same energy and each shows as its own line in the spectroscope.

### Reactions

Reactions run on sim time with Arrhenius kinetics. Units are `k_B = 1` and `T_room = 1`.

`Ea` is measured as the barrier's height **above the higher of the two states**. For a reaction with energy change `ΔU`:

- downhill rate = `A · exp(−Ea / T)`
- uphill rate = `A · exp(−(Ea + |ΔU|) / T)`

This gives detailed balance automatically, and `Ea = 0` is always legal. As a rule of thumb, a barrier of 5 is slowish (e^−5 ≈ 1/150 of A), a barrier of 15 is frozen, and a bond with `E = 8` is essentially permanent at room temperature but breaks readily at `T = 4`.

The reaction types are:

1. **Formation** (bimolecular). Two molecules with no color in common join with one new bond. The rate is `k · x_a · x_b`.
2. **Ring closure** (unimolecular). A bond forms between two atoms of the same molecule that aren't bonded yet.
3. **Breaking** (unimolecular). The reverse of 1 and 2: a bond breaks, leaving the molecule whole or splitting it in two.
4. **Displacement** (bimolecular, `Ea = 0`, one global prefactor, *displace A* in the Chemistry panel). A free single atom trades places with an atom bound in a molecule of two or more atoms that has no atom of its color, where one of the two is black, keeping the bond topology: free R, G or B displaces a bound K, as in `R–K + G ⇌ R–G + K` or `△RGK + B ⇌ △RGB + K`, and free K displaces a bound R, G or B, the same reactions the other way. Each direction has its own `ΔU`, from the species' energies. Two molecules never trade bound atoms, and a free atom never displaces anything from a molecule that already has its color. So displacement is fast recoloring through black, while formation is the slow construction of a shape, and the target's shape still has to be earned through formation.

S is in none of them: it neither bonds, nor displaces, nor is displaced. The network has 408 reactions (`ReactionNetwork.count`), as it had before S. The exact equilibrium solver carries S through untouched: conserved, alone, at no energy, though it counts in the mole fractions the rest balance by.

Each vessel's chemistry is stepped by explicit Euler along with everything else, every sim substep (at most 0.02 sim s), unless it's quiet (`ReactionNetwork.tick`). Each step works out how long the fluid takes to change appreciably: the shortest of how long any species in it would take to be used up by the reactions using it (gross, not net of the reverse, since that's what sets how far a step can go without overshooting), and how long the heat they give off would take to change their rates by a factor of e. Until 5% of that has gone by, up to 0.25 sim s, the vessel isn't stepped again, and then it's stepped through all the time it's owed at once. So no step moves more than about a tenth of any species, which follows the kinetics as closely as substeps do and is far too short to overshoot. It's stepped at once if the chemistry is edited, or if its temperature or any species' share of it moves its rates by more than 5% (fluid running in, a species appearing), in steps as long as the fluid as it is then allows, the first a single substep. Single colors, unreactive mixes and slow ones are stepped a few times a sim second; a mix of everything at room temperature every few substeps, and a hotter one more often, up to every substep.

### Heat

- **Reaction heat:** exotherms heat the fluid through a per-atom heat capacity.
- **Mixing:** heat adds, so temperatures average, weighted by atom count.
- **Heating:** besides exothermic reactions, the resistive heater (see **Tools**) heats whatever runs through it, and the swell bulbs' wire heats the little that's in them.
- **Cooling** (`src/game/cooling.ts`): fluid in every vessel (flasks, tanks, funnels, the instruments' tubes, the heater's tube, hose funnels) drifts exponentially to the room's temperature, T = 1, from above or below. Each sim second it closes 1 − e^(−rate · exposure) of the gap, where rate is 0.1 and exposure is a rough thermal surface per volume worked out from how the fluid stands as drawn: h deep, and w wide on average (its drawn area over its depth). Taking the fluid to be as deep front to back as it is wide, its floor and free surface give 2/h and its walls 4/w. So a thin layer, or a narrow column, loses heat fast, and a deep, wide pool slowly. A flask fills its wide base first; a tank fills straight up, a funnel up from its narrow stem, a pipette its tube and then its cup, and a swell bulb its bulb and then its neck; the heater's tube holds a shallow stream along its floor. All but 1/e of the gap goes in about 65 s for a full flask, 110 s for a full dispenser, 27 s for a full pipette, and 9 s for a tenth of a flask lying 2 deep across a dispenser's floor. Fluid in mid-air (streams, drops) doesn't cool. Faucets pour at T = 1, so faucet fluid just sits. Besides waiting, the ways to lower a temperature are diluting with faucet fluid, running endothermic reactions, or passing it through the heat exchanger against something cooler. The Chemistry panel's *Cooling* rows edit the rate and the ambient temperature; they aren't saved, and *Restore defaults* puts them back.

### Appearance

- **Color:** by default, a fluid looks like the atom-weighted mix of its colors, and the bond structure is invisible. This is lossy on purpose: R and G together average to a dull olive, for example, whether bonded or not. (The Appearance panel can try out colors that depend on bonds; see below.)
- **Clearness:** S is clear. It adds no color to the mix (the hue is the mix of the colored atoms alone), but thins the fluid: the more of its atoms are S, the more see-through it's drawn, by `solventAlpha` in `src/game/appearance.ts`: opacity 0.12 + 0.88 · (1 − share of S)^0.6, which falls slowly at first, so strong dilutions still show their color: about 0.70 at half S, 0.34 at nine tenths. Pure S is a faint, cool near-white at 0.12, still a liquid with a surface in its glass: a see-through fluid in a vessel is drawn darker toward its edges, where it's seen through more of itself, with a bright line along its surface, in its own color washed toward white (`fluid` in `src/game/sketch.ts`), as clear liquid looks; an opaque one is drawn as ever. Its faucet's knob is clear-white. Cold and heat darken and whiten it as any fluid.
- **Temperature:** each effect is a smooth function of T, with no cutoff where one effect takes over from another (see `src/game/appearance.ts`).
  - **Cold** fluids fade toward black. The color's brightness is scaled by `1 − e^(−3T)`: black at T = 0 and almost full brightness by T = 1.
  - **Hot** fluids wash out toward white, by `min(1, ln(1+T) / ln(101))²`: barely at T = 1, about a quarter of the way at T = 10, and fully at T = 100. That's the only sign of heat: nothing glows around a hot vessel.
  - God mode shows the actual number.
  - The **Appearance** debug panel tunes every constant in these curves live. *Copy values* puts the current settings on the clipboard as JSON, and *Restore defaults* undoes your changes.
- **Composition, experimentally:** the Appearance panel can also make color depend on bonds, so reactions show. A fluid is then the atom-weighted average of its molecules' colors, and each molecule's color comes from one of three models:
  - *current*, the default: a molecule is the average of its atoms, so the fluid is the average of its atoms and no reaction changes it;
  - *1: paint*: a molecule's atoms multiply like paints, rescaled to their average's brightness, so R–G is olive and R–B purple;
  - *2: light*: a molecule's atoms add like overlapping spotlights, clipped, so R–G is bright yellow and △RGB white.

  *Mix* blends between the average (0) and the full model (1). The *Cloudiness* checkbox makes bigger molecules more opaque: the fluid's opacity is the atom-weighted average over its colored atoms of an α for singles, pairs, and molecules of three or four atoms, times the solvent's thinning.

## The puzzle's chemistry

Blue bonds are uphill (E near −1 for now) and have no prefactor (A = 0), so they never form or break on their own, at any temperature. Blue gets into a molecule, or out of one, only by displacing black, or by free black displacing it. The target, the **△RGB triangle**, sits above R–G + B, so it never dominates an equilibrium.

Black bonds to red and green have a high barrier (Ea = 10), so at room temperature they hardly form or break, and it takes heat to bond black to anything or free it. That keeps a trace of free black from eating the target. Free black still displaces blue freely (displacement has no barrier), so each free black atom spoils one triangle into △RGK, freeing a blue. But that ring can't open cold, so the black stays stuck there instead of moving on to the next triangle. Hot, it does move on, and the damage keeps growing. The black–blue bond is uphill with A = 0 like blue's others.

The faucets pour single atoms only (and the solvent), so every molecule, the target included, has to be made by mixing. Blue can't be bonded to anything straight from its faucet, since blue bonds never form on their own: it gets in only by displacing black.

- **Pouring:** hold a flask and hold the right button to tip it, about its mouth, in 0.3 s: always toward its right (+x, right on the screen at the bench). Let go of the right button and it stands back up. It's worked out from the flask's inside cut into slabs 0.6 on a side, each one's volume spread evenly over the heights it spans, so the level rises smoothly with the fill, a trace included, rather than a row of slabs at a time. - **Guides:** while you hold something where it could be left, dashed streams show where fluid would go with it left right there, but only where it would land in something. Like real things, they're hidden behind anything in front of them, glass included, so one that disappears behind a flask is passing behind it, not into it. - **Placing** (`placement`, `onBench`, `magnet` and `blocked` in `src/game/engine.ts`): what's held follows the pointer across the bench's plane, by where it was taken hold of, and is shown just where it would stay if let go, so nothing has to be lined up by eye. A hose's nozzle over an inline instrument's tube snaps onto it (see **Hoses**). And anything nearly lined up, within 7 across (`MAGNET`), slides across to line up exactly: one of its spouts (or its nozzle) over the middle of the mouth it would pour into, or one of its mouths under a spout, nozzle or faucet that would pour into it, or a strip or a thermometer over the middle of a mouth it's over or down in; the nearest, if there are several. - **Carrying:** whatever you hold catches anything that falls into it, from a faucet, a spout, a drop or a spill, as anything else does, but only once you've held it still for 0.5 s (`CATCH_REST_S` in `src/game/still.ts`): until then, everything falls straight past it onto whatever's below, as if it weren't there, and is drawn so. So an empty flask carried under a stream stays empty, and one held still under it fills. It counts as moving just as it's picked up (so lifting a flask from under a faucet stops it filling), and again whenever where it would be left moves more than 0.5 across the wall from where it came to rest, which moving the view can do too; tipping a flask to pour doesn't, nor does the pointer shaking within that. It's your hand, so this goes by real seconds, paused or not. Left anywhere, it catches at once. What pours in from a hose going along with it (see Shift, below) lands in it all the same. A held tool keeps running, so carrying part of an assembly doesn't interrupt it: what leaves a spout with its hose out still comes out of the hose's nozzle, wherever that is, but what leaves a spout with no hose out goes straight down the drain (drawn falling to the floor), never into anything you carry it over. Picked up plainly (the left button or E), a thing is moved alone: the hoses pouring into it stay where they are, and its spouts with no hose out go with it. Picked up with **Shift** held (Shift + the left button, or Shift + E), it stays hooked up instead, as follows; touch screens pick things up plainly. Picking up a tool that way doesn't disconnect it: each of its spouts with no hose out that's letting something out (streaming, or gathering a drop) over an open top (but its own) has its hose pulled out as you pick it up, the nozzle left right where the spout was, held up by an arm, so it keeps pouring there. Q rolls those hoses back up as it puts the tool back, and so does leaving the tool with a spout right where its hose's nozzle is. Nor does picking something up with Shift cut off what pours into it: every faucet, hose's nozzle, or spout letting something out (streaming, or gathering a drop) whose stream lands in what you pick up (a flask, a tool's open tanks, or the flasks on a scale) has its hose, pulled out if it isn't, go along with it, its nozzle kept just where it was over it, so it keeps pouring in as you carry it. (An idle spout that happens to be over it is left be.) While carried, those nozzles have no arms and bump into nothing. When you leave it, each is left over the same spot of it, held up by an arm: as high over it as it was if there's room, or else as low as there is, from 15 over its mouth up to 300 (`followRests`). If one can't be, it can't be left there, and the note says so ("No room for a hose following it"). Q, or putting it away, puts those hoses back where they were, rolling up any pulled out for it. A hose's nozzle in hand lets nothing out without the right button: the tool or faucet it's pulled out of stops, hanging drops and all, until you press it.
- **Hoses:** every spout (a tool's, or a faucet's) has a hose rolled up inside it. Press the left button on a spout's tip, or the pipe just above it (on a faucet, its spout or the pipe down to it, not its knob), to pull its hose out: you hold its nozzle, a little metal tip, where the spout is, and a translucent tube droops from the spout to it as you carry it anywhere, showing what's running through it, bowed back toward the wall between its ends so that it hangs behind what's in the bench's plane rather than across its front. A click (or the end of a drag) leaves the nozzle where it is, held up by an arm out of the wall, as anything is left (see **Placing**): slid up to 7 across to be over the middle of a mouth under it, and not where it would overlap anything. A nozzle let go of over the top of an inline instrument's tube (the composition analyzer's, the spectroscope's, the bond visualizer's or a flow meter's), no more than 150 above it, snaps down onto it, centered (`docked` in `src/game/bodies.ts`), and needs no arm; held there, it's shown snapped on. From then on, whatever would leave the spout comes out of the nozzle instead, streaming or dripping into the first open top below it, so a spout can feed anything anywhere, even its own tool's tank. The left button (or E) on a nozzle takes hold of it again, where it is; on its spout, it brings the nozzle back to the spout from wherever it is (even out of view) and you hold it there, as if just pulled out, though Q still puts it back where it was; Backspace, holding it, rolls the hose back up into its spout. While you hold the nozzle, nothing comes out of it, nor out of the spout's tool's other spouts, without the right button (see **Carrying**). A hose is put away with its tool. Older saves' hoses, whose funnels hung under a spout or a faucet, become that spout's hose; those hung anywhere else are gone, with whatever was in their funnels.
- **Notes:** press **Enter** on anything that can be carried (a flask, a tool, a scale), or a faucet, to write a note about it: a text field opens where the note goes, and you type up to 24 characters, then Enter (pressed anew) or click anywhere else to keep it, or Escape to leave it as it was. An empty note clears it. Notes float above what they're about, always facing the view. A flask's note is its label, just above its mouth (and following it when it's carried). On a tool with one tank, the note is that tank's label, just above the tank, above the tool's own label if it has one; on the heat exchanger, Enter on a tank labels that tank, and anywhere else on it writes a note for the whole exchanger, above both; a thermometer or a wicking strip, which has no tank, has a note of its own, above its top. - **Tools** (`src/game/tools.ts`, and `src/game/bodies.ts` for where their parts are in the room) are glass tanks and pipes, built from their flat outlines on the old bench, and drawn from them: glass as a faint fill with pale edges, pipes in steel, cabinets light (as is the scale), and levers black. Only what's drawn of a tool counts as the tool (its glass included, however faint): the pointer passes through the empty space around its tanks, pipes and levers to whatever's behind. A tank's glass and rim count as the tank, not just the fluid in it, so an empty tank can be pointed at as one (for a note or the info panel), and the supply station's funnel counts as a tank of its own. Each has tanks on top that hold four flasks' worth (the funnels of the splitter, size sorter and heater, the instruments' tubes and the swell bulbs are smaller). Every vessel is drawn just big enough inside to hold what it holds, as much room per atom as a flask's inside (a flask holds a billion in about 63,000 cubic world units), so how much fluid each looks to have agrees from one to the next: each tank is as deep front to back as it needs to be for its outline (about 50 for a four-flask tank, 30 for a funnel, 6 for a pipette's tube), and its fluid settles into it as drawn, filling a funnel's narrow stem, a sump or a pipette's tube first. How fast a tank drains still goes by how high its fluid stands in its flat outline, as before. Each tank drains through its own valve, a round core with a lever on the tool's front face, and fluid leaves through one or more spouts on the bottom. Press the left button on a valve (its round core or its lever), or on the tool's face within 15 of a valve's center (about the length of its lever) but not on a spout, and drag: the lever points at the pointer, as it lies on the tool's front face, until the button's let go. A plain click there, with no drag, picks the tool up instead. Holding the right button on a valve, or within 15 of one, turns it the same way. Fully open is straight up from the valve, closed straight right, and partly open in between. A valve lets out its openness times the tank's full flow (1 flask/s), times how high the fluid stands as a share of the tank's height: a full tank pours at the full rate and a quarter-full one at a quarter of it. So a tank slows as it drains. Flat-bottomed tanks (the dispenser's, the exchanger's, the separator's and the big tank's) have a sump: a narrow point under the middle of the floor, with the valve at its tip, and the height counts from that tip. A thin layer across the floor still has the sump's depth behind it, so it keeps draining steadily, and once only the narrow point is wet, its level drops fast for how little is left, so the tank empties in a finite time instead of trickling on for ever. A full dispenser fully open is empty in about 14 s. The fluid is drawn filling the sump first. Only within 6 of the valve's center, its round core, does the lever stay put, since the angle there is too jumpy to aim with. Valves start closed. The right button anywhere else on a tool does nothing. Where valves crowd together, the nearest one turns. A dial (the heater's) turns the same way, pointing at the pointer: clockwise from down-left (off) to down-right (full), with straight below going to the nearer end.
  - **Spouts** pour into the first open top straight below them (or below their hose's nozzle, if it's pulled out; see **Hoses**), in a stream whose width goes as the square root of its flow. Below 5M atoms/s (0.005 flask/s of single atoms) an outlet drips instead: what leaves it gathers in a hanging drop, which falls as a Poisson process whose rate, e^((atoms − size) / spread) per second, rises without limit as the drop grows: by default (size 1.5M, spread 0.12M, both set in the Chemistry panel) it's 0.015 per second at 1M atoms, 1 at 1.5M and 64 at 2M, so drops come out a fairly regular size however fast they're fed. Once it lets go, a drop falls under gravity into the first open top below. Hanging drops are saved; drops in mid-air aren't, and neither kind reacts. The open top can be a flask, including one you're holding still under the spout (see **Carrying**), or another tool's tank: anything whose mouth is under the spout, front to back as well as across. If there isn't one, the fluid falls to the counter or the floor and drains away. A stream's width shows everything that left the outlet over the whole frame, so a wide-open valve that drains its tank faster than it's fed still shows its stream at any sim speed.
- **Overflow:** fluid pouring into a full vessel (from a spout, a drop, a faucet, a hose or your own pouring) still goes in and mixes, and the same amount of the mixture spills over the rim: off the right of a flask's lip, or over the right lip of a tank or funnel, as it faces. It falls as a stream, into the first open top below, which may overflow in turn, or down the drain. So a flask of blue under a stream of red turns redder and redder while purple, then red, pours off it. Faucets keep running into a full vessel, too. Chemistry that swells a fluid (by molecules) spills the same way.
  - **Dispenser:** one tank, drained through one spout.
  - **Tank:** a big dispenser: one tank holding a hundred flasks, an upright glass cylinder drawn as big as a hundred flasks are (200 across and 201 tall, in world units where a flask is 70 tall), over a round sump with a spout under it, and its valve out at the front of the tank, on a stem from the pipe under it, so the tank's lower edge doesn't hide it. Ten thin, faint rings round it mark each tenth of what it holds, the last at the brim. Like any tank it pours 1 flask/s full and fully open, in proportion to how high its fluid stands.
  - **Pipette:** a narrow dispenser holding a tenth of a flask, filled through a little funnel on top (whose mouth is what catches streams and drops, and spills over its right lip when full). Its fluid is drawn across tube and funnel together, by area, so it fills the tube first and a full pipette's funnel is full to the brim. Ten graduation marks up its left wall show each tenth of its capacity: evenly up the tube for the first seven, then closer together up the widening funnel, the last at the brim, with the half and the full marks longer. Its tube and funnel are square-sectioned glass, and the marks are printed flat on their front faces, leaning forward with the funnel's, not standing off them. Full and fully open, its valve lets out 0.02 flask/s, a fifth of what it holds each second. Its fluid's height, which sets that flow, runs up its narrow tube and then its cup, so the flow drops off quickly while the cup drains, then more slowly down the tube.
  - **Separator:** one tank, drained through a manifold with five spouts, 100 apart so a flask or a tool's tank fits under each. It sorts molecules by the share of their atoms that are black, and each has its own spout, left to right: none (R, R–G, △RGB, and the solvent), one in four (any of four atoms), one in three (△RGK, R–G–K), half (R–K), all (K). By default it's perfect: every molecule goes out its own spout. Below that, a molecule goes to spout j in proportion to e^(−sharpness · (j − its own)²), so at sharpness 3 it goes 91% out its own spout if that's one of the middle three, or 95% at either end, and nearly all the rest out the spouts next to it. The Chemistry panel's *Separator* slider sets the sharpness from 0 (an even five-way split) to 10, the default, which is perfect, showing what share each spout keeps; it isn't saved, and *Restore defaults* puts it back. It splits whole molecules, and heat goes with the atoms. A small handle on one end of the manifold shows which way round it is (it starts on the left), without saying which side gets what.
  - **Heat exchanger:** two tanks, A and B, 100 apart, whose streams pass each other in counterflow through two hoses wound into a double helix. Each stream crosses over and leaves from the spout under the *other* tank, and the hoses show each stream's color. They trade heat but never mix, and heat is conserved. It uses the standard effectiveness–NTU model with a fixed exchange capacity (`EXCHANGE_RATE`, 2 flasks/s): the slower stream gets a fraction ε of the way to the other's inlet temperature. Two equal streams at 0.25 flask/s (valves at a quarter, tanks full) nearly swap temperatures (ε = 8/9). At 1 flask/s they get ε = 2/3. If one valve is shut, the other stream passes through unchanged.

  - **Wicking strip** (`strip`): a strip of bare cream blotting paper, 12 across and 108 long, hanging from a little dark clip at its top, which the arm holding it up grips once it's left (in your hands it's just the paper and its clip, as a thermometer is just its rod), its foot cut to a point (its last 6 narrowing to it), so it's plain which end goes in. Nothing is marked on it. It has no tanks, spouts or valves, and, like a thermometer's rod, only its top (the clip and the top of the paper) bumps into anything, so it can stand down in a flask (it's narrow enough to go down a flask's neck, 18 across inside, with room to spare) or a tool's tank, though it's never sunk into the counter. It's a dipper, as the thermometers are, and it's dipped by hand: there's no button for it. Carried, it's in a vessel only once its foot has come down into it through its mouth (or open top), and until its foot leaves the vessel's inside again, so sweeping it across the bench through flasks and tanks touches nothing; while it's in one, it's in that vessel's fluid wherever its foot is under the surface (`heldDip` and `dipping` in `src/game/engine.ts`, `insideVessel` in `src/game/bodies.ts`). It can't be left with its foot inside a vessel it didn't go into by the top ("It goes in through the top"). Left anywhere, held up by its arm, it's in whatever fluid its foot is in; let go of over a mouth or down in one, it slides up to 7 across to stand over its middle (see **Placing**). It's used once, in two steps: spot, then carry. Its foot coming into a fluid (from being in none) while the paper has yet to drink anything touches it: the paper takes one spot of it, a thousandth of a flask (a tenth of what the paper holds wet from end to end), at once, or what there is if that's less, spread evenly over the stretches of it that are under the surface (and at least the lower half of its point, touched by hand), up to three spots' worth in all, so a first touch in plain solvent doesn't use up its only chance. So one in hand takes its spot as deep as it's dipped, and one left standing in a fluid as deep as it stands. A spot that has yet to move is drawn stretch by stretch, not blended, so it fills the tip of the point up to a flat top, where the surface was. Then it drinks what its foot is in, the carrier (a faucet's atom does), while it's left standing in it, or once it's been held in it for 0.35 real seconds from the touch (`DIP_SOAK` in `src/game/engine.ts`; real time, being how long you hold it there, so it feels the same at any sim speed): so a quick dip is a spot, a held one a soak, and a strip left standing in a vessel soaks. While it drinks, the paper draws the carrier up, quickly at first and ever more slowly, as paper wicks: what it's drunk grows as the square root of the time, so it's soaked half its length in 5 sim s and the whole in 20, but only as far as the vessel supplies it, since soaking a share of the length draws that share of a hundredth of a flask from it. Lifted out, or if what it stands in runs dry, it stops where it is, and put back, it goes on. Nothing is poured onto it, so nothing floods it. The paper is modelled as 108 stretches, one unit each, from its foot to its top, the spot on the first few, those that were in the fluid. Each species has a run, 1 − drag / 34 (`STRIP_RUN` in `src/game/tools.ts`), where its drag is its weight (see **Atoms and molecules**) plus the weight of its loose ends again: the atoms held by only one bond (`src/chem/shape.ts`), which snag in the paper. A liquid is its molecules, so what's drawn up goes at its own pace, the mean run of what's drawn, by volume (`leadOf`), and each species on the paper travels its own run over that of the liquid's way, or all of it if that's more than 1: so the carrier goes with the liquid, what drags more lags it in proportion, and nothing outruns it. Each moves on up a whole stretch at a time: each time it's due, all of it on each stretch moves on into the next, but for 5% left behind to move on later, in whole molecules, never past the share of the length the paper has soaked (what's left behind makes it lag, so it moves on a stretch for each 0.95 of one it travels). So a spot's species end up as narrow bands with a soft tail below, each min(1, its run / the carrier's) of the way up from the foot, broader the further they've run, but only by what's left behind: after n moves a band is about √(n · 0.05 · 0.95) stretches wide (as a standard deviation), so the triangle's, carried by S, after about 75, is 1.9 stretches wide, under 2% of the length (a little more for the three stretches its spot started over). So where bands land depends a little on the carrier. Single atoms run freest: the solvent S (0.94), then G (0.91), B (0.88), K (0.85) and R (0.82); a ring, with no loose ends, runs further than a chain of the same atoms; and a chain with heavy ends lags one with light ends. The triangle △RGB's run is 0.62, and the three chains of its atoms' 0.41 (G–R–B), 0.35 (R–B–G) and 0.32 (R–G–B). Carried by the solvent S, the triangle goes 0.66 as far as the liquid, ending 0.66 of the way up from the foot, and the chains 0.44, 0.38 and 0.34 as far, ending as far up; carried by R, the triangle goes 0.75 as far, and the chains 0.50, 0.43 and 0.39, ending as far up. Either way a batch with chains in it shows them as bands of their own, below the triangle's; and anything spotted that runs more freely than the carrier (G, carried by R) rides with the liquid to the top. Different species can still run the same distance (R–G and B–K weigh the same, for one), where their bands lie over each other. Stand a fresh strip in a sample itself, so its spot is the sample too, and each of the sample's species climbs from the foot at its own pace beside the mixture's, as a staircase of fronts rather than bands; touch it to the sample and then stand it in a carrier, as in a dish of S (see **Dish**), and you get bands. The paper is darker where it's wet, and wet is what's on it: there's no waterline, since a liquid is its molecules. Each stretch is drawn as wet as what it holds over its share of what the whole paper holds wet through (a 108th of a hundredth of a flask), as far as fully (`wetness` in `src/game/tools.ts`), blended smoothly from one stretch's middle to the next, and the stretch it draws up at as wet as the next (it fills, and empties as what's on it moves on). So it's wet as far as the carrier has come, and dry past it, the edge as soft as the carrier's own there; where there's next to nothing, it's next to dry. Each species on each stretch shows as ink of its own color (its atoms' colors averaged, as a fluid's is), 1 − e^(−33 · v / spot) opaque for its volume v there, blended smoothly from one stretch to the next: so a twentieth of the spot shows clearly at its band's peak after a full run, about 0.35 to 0.45 opaque, on clean paper between the bands, while what's most of the spot is as dark as it gets near its band's middle: the paper shows what's there more than how much. When it has soaked its whole length it's done: it takes up nothing more, whatever it stands in, and it never dries, so everything on it stays just as it is, its carrier and its wetness too, a record to lift out and stand anywhere, to keep and compare, and to label with Enter; Backspace throws it away. Single atoms only tint the paper, at 0.4 of what a molecule of two or more atoms would, so a carrier is seen climbing, and bands of molecules show over it. Nothing ever leaves the paper, so a sample of nothing but single atoms keeps its bands, and a strip stood in a colored atom straight from a faucet is left tinted that color. What's on the paper doesn't react or cool. Fluid hotter than T = 2 browns the paper that's in it, and from T = 6 chars it through: only the stretches under its surface, which are a spot's own when it's touched to it, so the scorch ends at the same flat line, since what the paper takes up cools at once. Each stretch stays as scorched as the hottest fluid it's been in left it, so a spot from a hot sample leaves a charred tip and clean paper above it, whatever carrier then runs up it; and a strip stood in hot fluid chars as far as it stands in it. Nothing on it is labeled. Printed across both edges of the paper are fine dark lines dividing its length into 20 equal parts (`STRIP.marks`), 1.8 in from each edge and every fifth 3.2, with none at its ends, to read off how far up it something has run; they show over what's on the paper, and under where it's scorched. What's on the paper, stretch by stretch, how much it has drunk and how scorched each stretch is are saved with the lab (an older save's strip kept one number for how scorched it was, which goes for as much of it as it had soaked). Older saves' strips, which stood in a tray of their own, load as bare strips, the tray and whatever was in it gone, and the tray's label their note.

  - **Column** (`column`): the one tool that separates molecules by their shape, slowly and by hand, a little at a time. With the funnel empty or the tap shut, everything on the bed stays where it is, so a band can be parked partway down. To use it: pour a little sample into the funnel and let it run in, then pour in a carrier (any fluid; the solvent S, being clear, is the cleanest), and catch what comes out, swapping flasks under the spout as each species arrives, the fast first; or pour the sample in continuously, and the first of its species to come out is the fastest alone, until the next breaks through. Through the glass, the bed shows what's on each stretch by the wicking strip's ink (so single atoms tint it, and a clear carrier leaves it pale), for as much of a stretch of the strip's paper as it would take up of one of the bed's, blended smoothly from one stretch to the next, so bands are seen travelling down: dry packing is the paper's cream, and wet, a little darker, as wet as what's on each stretch, as on the strip (an 80th of a fifth of a flask wets a stretch through), so it's wet as far down as the liquid has come, and no further. Fine dark rings are marked right round the bed, dividing its length into 20 equal parts (`COLUMN.marks`), every fifth a little over twice as thick, with none at its ends, to read off how far down something has run; they show over what's on the bed. What's on the bed doesn't react or cool, and keeps its heat. What's on it, stretch by stretch, and how much it has drunk are saved with the lab.
  - **Sleeper culture** (`culture`): a plate of living cells from the ship's stores, to try a fluid on before it goes near the passengers. It's a flat glass cell, 40 across and 46 tall and only a couple of units deep, in a dark frame over a dark backing, filled through a funnel on top (its mouth 20 across) that fans out to the plate's width; it holds a tenth of a flask, and drains through a valve and a spout under it, a fifth of a flask a second full and fully open, in proportion to how high its fluid stands, as any valve does, so it's empty in a few seconds. On the inside of its front stand about 180 cells, in rows 3.2 apart, each row half a step out of line with the last: a pale blue dot with a bright middle while a cell is alive. The fluid in the plate harms the cells it covers, those below its surface as it's drawn, and no others, so how far the plate is filled is how many cells are tried. It harms them by its loose ends (`harm` in `src/game/tools.ts`): the atoms held by only one bond, per molecule, a lone atom counting as one, but the solvent as none: it does them no harm, and dilutes what does, since harm is per molecule. So rings of any kind do them no harm, a chain counts two, and single atoms one each; a twentieth of the molecules being chains is a tenth of a loose end per molecule. Heat harms them too: each unit of temperature above 2 counts as another fifth of a loose end. Each cell in the fluid dies at random, whatever the others do, at a rate of 0.23 a sim second for each unit of harm (`CULTURE.rate`): so half of those in the fluid are dead in 3 sim s in anything straight from a faucet, in 15 s where a tenth of the molecules are chains, in 30 s for a twentieth, and in two and a half minutes for a hundredth; and in the target alone, none, ever. (How many die in a step is their number times the chance of it, rounded at random, like everything else.) A dying cell swells, amber, for 1.2 sim s, and is a small brown dot from then on, for good, whatever's in the plate afterwards. So how fast the brown spreads says how harmful the fluid is, and given long enough, anything with a loose end in it kills every cell it covers. Nothing says what killed them. Drained, a plate keeps its cells just as they were, living and dead, as a record of what was tried on it, to stand beside others and label with Enter; cells that the fluid still covers go on dying while it drains, the lowest longest. A drained plate can be filled again, to try its living cells on something else; Backspace throws it away. Which cells are dead is saved with the lab.
  - **Thermometers** (`thermometer` and `thermometerWide`): two of them, made from an equipment wheel as many times as you like, and put away like any tool. Each is an upright glass rod, 150 long and 5 across, with rounded ends: a bulb of red spirit, 9 long, at its foot; a fine glass capillary up it from the bulb; and, along its upper part, a white enamel backing inside the glass, 96 long, with the scale printed on it. The scale is linear (`thermometerAt` in `src/game/tools.ts`), from 0 at its foot, 44 above the rod's foot, to the top of its range 80 higher: the fine one reads 0 to 5, with a tick every 0.1, a longer one every 0.5, and a number at every whole number; the wide one reads 0 to 25, with a tick every 0.5, a longer one every 2.5, and a number every 5. The ticks are right of the capillary and the numbers at the backing's right edge, and nothing else is written on it. The column of spirit stands as high as the bulb's temperature, up to the top of the capillary, which runs on past the scale's top a tenth of its length again. It has no tanks, spouts or valves: it's carried, and left anywhere on the bench, held up by an arm near its top like any tool (in your hands it has none), but only the top 24 of it bumps into anything, so it can stand down in a flask or a tool's tank (or anywhere else, though never sunk into the counter). Its bulb takes the temperature of the fluid it's in (`bathAt` in `src/game/bodies.ts`): an upright flask's, if the bulb's inside its outline and below its fluid's surface as drawn (not one tipped to pour), or a tool's tank's, if it's within the tank's walls as drawn (a swell bulb's bulb or neck) and below its fluid's surface; not the supply station's, which are in the wall, nor a falling stream. In fluid, the bulb closes all but 1/e of the gap to its temperature each sim second; in the air, or in an empty vessel, it drifts to the room's temperature over 8 sim s. That holds whether it's held or left. It's a dipper, like the wicking strip, and dipped the same way, by hand: carried, its bulb is in a vessel's fluid only once it has come down into the vessel through its mouth (see **Wicking strip**), and left standing in a vessel, it reads whatever its bulb is in, at whatever depth it was left. If its bulb gets hotter than its range by a tenth (5.5 for the fine one, 27.5 for the wide one), the spirit has nowhere left to go and it shatters, for good, with a sharp crack of glass (see **Sound**): its bulb and column are gone, the rod is left snapped off 24 above its foot, its end jagged, and it reads nothing from then on, though it can still be carried and put away. It's the bulb's own temperature that counts, not the fluid's, so a quick dip in something too hot can be survived: from the room's temperature, the fine one lasts about 0.8 sim s in fluid at 9 before it shatters. Its bulb's temperature, and whether it's broken, are saved with the lab. An older save's thermometer (when it was an inline instrument with a tube) loads as a fine thermometer, without its tube and its hose.
  - **Composition analyzer** (`analyzer`): there's exactly one. Every lab starts with it (on the bench just past the supply station, 2560 along the wall, its spouts 178 above the counter, unless a preset places it), it isn't on an equipment wheel, and it can't be put away; older saves get one, and lose the mass spectrometer it replaced. It's an inline instrument: a narrow upright glass tube (a twentieth of a flask, 10 across and 40 tall inside) whose top a hose's nozzle snaps onto (see **Hoses**), and which anything else falling into it goes into too. The tube drains straight through at 2 flasks/s however full it is, so it only fills (and spills) if more arrives than that. At its foot it lets 99% of what passes out the spout under it, and draws off the other 1% into a cabinet beside it, about 170 wide, which lets it out a waste spout on its far side, 200 along from the tube. A thin thread of color down the tube's middle shows what's running through. The cabinet has an old green-on-black screen, with no words anywhere, and no button: it reads continuously. The screen has four triangles in a row, each standing on its base, for molecules of 1, 2, 3 and 4 atoms, left to right. Each is cut through the middles of its sides into four smaller triangles, one per color: G's at the top, R's at the bottom left, B's at the bottom right, and K's, upside down, in the middle. There are no lines between the sectors, just their glow. Each sector glows as bright as the share, by volume, of what the cabinet drew off lately that molecules of its size with an atom of its color in them make up. So a stream of R lights 1R fully, R–G lights 2R and 2G fully, and △RGB all three of its sectors; a stream of 20% R, 20% G, 30% B and 30% R–G lights 1R and 1G at 0.2, 1B at 0.3, and 2R and 2G at 0.3 each. The more mixed the stream, the dimmer and harder to read the screen. It has no sector for the solvent and is blind to it, but S still takes up room in what the shares are of, so a diluted stream reads dimmer: half R, half S lights 1R at half. Lately means a moving average that fades the past smoothly: each moment counts e^(−age / 0.5 sim s) as much as now (`INSTRUMENT_TAU` in `src/game/tools.ts`), so a change starts to show at once and comes in fully over a second or two, rather than all at once a fixed time later. Once what it draws off has faded to under a thousand molecules a sim second, the screen goes dark. It reads on sim time, like everything else, so it freezes while paused; held, it keeps running like any tool (see **Carrying**). While it has something to read, the cabinet hums quietly (`src/game/rumble.ts`, synthesized with Web Audio).
  - **Spectroscope** (`spectroscope`): there's exactly one. Every lab starts with it (on the bench 300 right of the composition analyzer, unless a preset places it), it isn't on an equipment wheel, and it can't be put away; older saves get one. It's an inline instrument like the analyzer, with the same tube a hose's nozzle snaps onto and the same cabinet beside it, but it lets everything through, out the one spout under the tube, and draws nothing off. Its green-on-black screen is a graph of the bonds passing through lately (averaged as the composition analyzer's reading is, fading smoothly), by their energy: x is E, the size of a bond's energy, in uB, on a log scale from about 0.6 to 33 (e^−0.5 to e^3.5), labeled E (uB), along a baseline halfway up the screen, with ticks on it as on log paper: at 1 to 9 times each power of ten, those at the powers of ten (1 and 10) longer, and numbered along the screen's foot. y is f, how many of those bonds passed, in S (a million bonds a sim second, so 1 mS is a thousand), labeled above the plot with an SI prefix to suit the range shown, as f (mS) or f (kS), up from the baseline for a stable bond (E > 0, which absorbs) and down from it for an uphill one (E < 0, which gives energy back), at its size. Its scale is logarithmic and ranges itself to what's passing: the top (and bottom) of the plot is √10 times the tallest line, peak or dip, and it reaches four powers of ten below that, so a line ten thousand times weaker than the strongest still shows as a small spike. Precisely, f stands ln(1 + f / floor) over ln(1 + top / floor) of the way up, where floor is the top over 10⁴: a plain log scale well above the floor, easing to the baseline at 0, so a line meets the baseline smoothly and a strong one is a rounded hill whose foot widens the more there is of it, not a box. Ticks run along the left as on log paper, the same both ways from the baseline (marked 0), the powers of ten longer and numbered, in a smaller type than the axes' titles, where there's room: 1, 10, 100 and .1, .01, .001 of the unit. The prefix (n, u, m, none, k, M or G) is the multiple of three powers of ten nearest the middle of the powers of ten the axis shows, so no number runs past a thousand or a thousandth (`spectroscopeUnit` in `src/game/tools.ts`). So the tallest line always stands at the same height, and changing the flow slides the ticks instead: ten times the flow, and every number moves down a step, a thousand times, and the prefix moves up one. Each kind of bond is a Gaussian, 0.02 wide (its standard deviation in lnE), and lines that overlap add up before they're put on the scale; where a peak and a dip overlap, the graph is the one less the other (`spectroscopeAt` in `src/game/tools.ts`). Single atoms have no bonds, so they show nothing; with nothing passing, the lines keep their height while the f axis's numbers fall with the fading average, and the graph goes flat, its f ticks gone, once under a thousand bonds a sim second are left in it. It follows the Chemistry panel: change a bond's energy and its spike moves.
  - **Bond visualizer** (`visualizer`): there's exactly one. Every lab starts with it (on the bench 300 right of the spectroscope, unless a preset places it), it isn't on an equipment wheel, and it can't be put away; older saves get one. It's an inline instrument like the spectroscope, with the same tube a hose's nozzle snaps onto and the same cabinet beside it, and it too lets everything through, out the one spout under the tube, drawing nothing off. Its green-on-black screen, with no words anywhere, has four points, one per atom, placed as in the composition analyzer's triangles: G at the top of a triangle standing on its base, R at its bottom left, B at its bottom right, and K in its middle. For each bond there can be, a line joins its two atoms' points, glowing as bright as the share, by volume, of what passed through lately that has that bond in it (`bondShares` in `src/game/tools.ts`), averaged as the composition analyzer's reading is, fading the past smoothly (`INSTRUMENT_TAU`). So a stream of R–G lights the line from R to G fully, △RGB lights the triangle of R, G and B, and half R, half R–G lights the R–G line half as bright; single atoms light nothing. Its six lines are the triangle's three sides and the three from its middle out to its corners, so no two cross. The points always show; once what passes has faded to under a thousand molecules a sim second, the lines go dark.

Presets are defined in `src/game/presets.ts`, and can pull out hoses, naming the spout each comes from and the tank its nozzle goes over. - **Chemistry table:** every parameter is live-editable. Drag a number sideways to scale it by 1% per pixel (100 px ≈ ×e). Double-click a bond energy `E` to flip its sign, or any other number to type it, which is how to set an `A` back to 0. *Restore defaults* puts the default chemistry back, volume back to counting molecules, and the drip, heater, cooling, separator and size sorter settings back too. All reaction rates rebuild on every change.
- **Saving** (`src/game/save.ts`): the lab (every flask, tool, scale and hose, where each is on the wall, their contents (a heater's tube included), valves, tares and notes, a thermometer's bulb's temperature and whether it's broken, what's on a wicking strip, what's on a column's bed and how far it has soaked, which of a sleeper culture's cells are dead, and what the supply station is fitted for and how far through its changeover it is), whether you've been told you can nap, where the view is at the bench (`view`), and the chemistry parameters are saved to local storage every 2 seconds and when you leave the page, and restored on load. Positions across and up are stored as on the old flat bench (see **The lab**), and the save says `wall: true`. A save without it, from when the lab was a room to walk round or from before, is laid out afresh along the wall as it loads (`flatten` in `src/game/flatten.ts`): the one-of-a-kind instruments in a row past the supply station, flasks and scales along the counter, and tools hung in rows above it, nothing overlapping anything, with the same plumbing. Every spout, faucet and hose's nozzle still pours into the vessel it poured into, through a hose pulled out where it needs one, one that poured into nothing still does (its hose's nozzle, if it had one, parked on the counter), and no new link is made; and every thermometer or wicking strip that stood in something stands as deep in the same vessel. Where things stood in the room, which way they faced and where the player stood aren't kept. Anything outside the room is brought inside it. *Export* copies the whole setup as a string (base64 of JSON), and *Import* loads one. Contents are stored by species name, so saves survive reordering the species list. Sticky notes in saves from when there was a pencil are ignored.

## Open questions / next steps

- **Separation.** The separator and size sorter split species only partly. Candidates for more:
  - boiling: small species are volatile, which reuses temperature;
  - a size sieve: singles pass, everything bigger stays;
  - a color trap: an item that binds one free color.

  The choice affects what shape the target should be.
- **More instruments.** The thermometer, swell bulbs, wicking strips, sleeper culture and the station's needle came out of one brainstorm (and dipping a strip straight into a vessel, like a thermometer, is built now); these from it aren't built:
  - ringing glass, which was built and then taken out again: a key that flicked the flask or tank looked at, which rang with the notes of what was in it. Each molecule's notes were those of its atoms on its bonds as springs (the atoms' weights, the bonds' energies as stiffness), brought down into hearing, each species' as loud as the square root of its share, so a chain among triangles was a stray note a tritone under them; and the glass's own notes fell as the vessel filled. It was shelved because the long ring it needs to be read by ear isn't how a full vessel sounds, which rings lower but duller. The code is in the history, at the commit that took it out;
  - stain tubes: a tube snapped onto a nozzle, whose stain creeps along in proportion to the loose ends that have passed, in a tint per end color, so a trace adds up over a whole flask;
  - witness coupons: a slip hung in a tank that lays down a band of tarnish each sim minute by how much free black there was, to read after sleeping;
  - a frost point: a slow chill in which a pure fluid frosts sharply and a mixture later, and the mixed test (reference, sample, and both together); it needs a chiller (below);
  - drop size by composition, so two pipettes dripping side by side drift out of step;
  - a seed crystal that grows in layers, clear when pure and specked by molecules of the same atoms in another shape;
  - froth whose bubbles are regular only for a single species, and a glow while something's reacting;
  - a getter cartridge that takes up free black until it's full (it would also purify, which changes the puzzle).
- **Temperature control.** The resistive heater heats, and everything cools to the room. Nothing chills below room temperature yet except endotherms; an ice bath would. The *Temperature range* preset's flasks now drift to room temperature over a minute or two.
- **More glassware and tools.** Graduated cylinders and pipettes.
- **Tool follow-ups.**
  - A tank can only be emptied through its spout.
  - Tools can't stand on the scale, so a dispenser's contents can't be weighed.
- **Tuning.**
  - The displacement prefactor: if it's too high, recoloring is trivial and the whole puzzle is getting the topology right.
  - Heat capacity: R + G alone heats up noticeably.
  - Whether the four colors stay distinguishable in mixtures, and black from a cold fluid, which also fades to black.
  - The solvent makes hot size-sorting easier: a chain split apart hot doesn't find its pieces again as soon, diluted, so they can be sorted before they rejoin. Its cost in volume (what it takes up, and has to be separated out again) and in heat (what it takes to heat it with the rest) is the lever on how easy that is.
  - The four-atom chemistry's defaults are ported from the six-atom one by analogy (black in yellow's place) and not yet tuned into a verified puzzle.
- **Playtester notes** (not yet acted on):
  - Make sure there's a synthesis pathway: a route that actually reaches more than 95% cryostabilizer.
  - Maybe add an off switch to the new device. The note doesn't say which device is meant.
  - Maybe add a mixer.
  - Add some sort of UI tutorial on how to change valve positions (press on a valve and drag: its lever points at the pointer).

## Development

```sh
npm install
npm run dev        # local dev server
npm test           # chemistry tests (vitest)
npm run build      # typecheck + production build into dist/
```

Code layout:

- `src/chem/`: the chemistry model, pure TypeScript with no DOM. It covers atoms, species enumeration, parameters, and the reaction network with its integrator.
- `src/game/`: flasks, tools, the scale, pouring, fluid color, and `GameEngine`, which owns the lab: the view, input, carrying and placing (with `physics.ts`'s overlaps) and the simulation loop. `view.ts` draws it, flat, on a 2D canvas, `bodies.ts` says where each thing's parts are in the room, and `flatten.ts` lays an older save out along the wall.
- `src/components/`: the React UI around the canvas: speed control, the equipment wheels, the corner list of actions, the touch screens' buttons, the chemistry table and the god-mode panel.

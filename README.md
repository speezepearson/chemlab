# Slurry Lab

A first-person chemistry-lab puzzle game set in a world with much simpler chemistry than ours. You walk around a lab handling fluids in glassware, and the goal is to work out how to synthesize more of a scarce target fluid.

**Premise** (see the intro): you're Nadia Hassan, a passenger on a Celestia Starlines colony ship to Mu Ceti, woken from cryosleep after something hit the ship and took out the bridge, the crew and nearly all the cryostabilizer. The 32,210 passengers still asleep need 0.0003499 mL each a day of it to last the 4,456 days (12.2 years) left: 50.22 L; there are 28.10 L. The target species is the cryostabilizer, so you have to figure out how to make more.

The faucets are unlimited, so the target is scarce because you don't know how to make it, not because you lack raw material. The puzzle is discovering a synthesis route.

## The world's physics

### Fluids

A packet of fluid, such as the contents of a flask, is fully described by:

- its **temperature**, and
- its **count of each molecular species**.

There is **no solvent**. Reaction rates depend on mole fractions, so only ratios matter, not absolute amounts. Adding fluid gradually still matters, because it keeps one reagent scarce while it goes in. There is no "add water" lever.

Volume is measured in molecules, so a flask's capacity is a number of molecules: a billion. Capacity, fill level, pouring and flow all go by molecules, so bonding shrinks a fluid and breaking bonds swells it, and whatever no longer fits spills over the rim. A checkbox in the Chemistry panel counts atoms instead. Mass, heat capacity, reaction rates, the god-mode percentages and the tester's purity test stay per atom either way. The setting isn't saved, and *Restore defaults* turns molecule counting back on.

Every count in a vessel is a whole number of molecules, and its heat is a whole number of energy quanta (one unit of bond energy each). Temperature is derived as heat / (heat capacity × atoms), so mixing simply adds heat, and energy is conserved exactly, except what the heater adds and what's lost to the room (see **Heat**). Whenever the expected result is fractional, such as reaction events in a step, one species' share of a pour, or the separator's split, it's rounded at random: up with probability equal to the fraction. That keeps averages exact, so slow reactions still happen. JavaScript numbers hold whole numbers exactly up to 2⁵³ ≈ 9×10¹⁵, so plain numbers are enough and BigInt isn't needed. Faucet recipes are the one fractional thing: one atom's worth, poured out as whole molecules. Counts that large behave like continuous quantities, so no small-number artifacts show up. The chemistry doesn't care about the scale, since everything in it depends on ratios.

### Atoms and molecules

- **Six atoms:** the primary and secondary colors R, G, B, C, M, Y.
- **Three opposed pairs:** R/C, G/M and B/Y. Each pair is a *group*.
- **What a molecule is:** a connected graph of atoms with at most one atom from each group. No molecule can hold two Rs, or both R and C. A molecule therefore has at most three atoms.
- **Species count:** 6 singles + 12 pairs + (8 color-triples × 4 connected shapes: 3 paths and a triangle) = **50 species**. That is small enough to simulate exactly.

### Bonds

Each of the 12 bondable color pairs has three parameters:

- `E`: the bond energy. Positive means stable.
- `Ea`: the activation energy.
- `A`: the Arrhenius prefactor.

Opposite colors never bond. A species' energy is `U = −Σ E` over its bonds.

### Reactions

Reactions run on sim time with Arrhenius kinetics. Units are `k_B = 1` and `T_room = 1`.

`Ea` is measured as the barrier's height **above the higher of the two states**. For a reaction with energy change `ΔU`:

- downhill rate = `A · exp(−Ea / T)`
- uphill rate = `A · exp(−(Ea + |ΔU|) / T)`

This gives detailed balance automatically, and `Ea = 0` is always legal. As a rule of thumb, a barrier of 5 is slowish (e^−5 ≈ 1/150 of A), a barrier of 15 is frozen, and a bond with `E = 8` is essentially permanent at room temperature but breaks readily at `T = 4`.

The reaction types are:

1. **Formation** (bimolecular). Two molecules with disjoint groups join with one new bond. The rate is `k · x_a · x_b`.
2. **Ring closure** (unimolecular). A 3-atom path becomes a triangle.
3. **Breaking** (unimolecular). The reverse of 1 and 2.
4. **Swap** (bimolecular, `Ea = 0`, one global prefactor). Any two species holding opposite colors in the same slot trade those atoms, for example `RG + C ⇌ CG + R`. Swaps between bound atoms are allowed too. A swap relabels atoms but keeps the bond topology. So swapping is fast recoloring, while formation is the slow construction of a shape, and the target's shape still has to be earned through formation.

### Heat

- **Reaction heat:** exotherms heat the fluid through a per-atom heat capacity.
- **Mixing:** heat adds, so temperatures average, weighted by atom count.
- **Heating:** besides exothermic reactions, the resistive heater (see **Tools**) heats whatever runs through it.
- **Cooling** (`src/game/cooling.ts`): fluid in every vessel (flasks, tanks, funnels, the spectrometer's cup, the heater's tube, hose funnels) drifts exponentially to the room's temperature, T = 1, from above or below. Each sim second it closes 1 − e^(−rate · exposure) of the gap, where rate is 0.1 and exposure is a rough thermal surface per volume worked out from how the fluid stands as drawn: h deep, and w wide on average (its drawn area over its depth). Taking the fluid to be as deep front to back as it is wide, its floor and free surface give 2/h and its walls 4/w. So a thin layer, or a narrow column, loses heat fast, and a deep, wide pool slowly. A flask fills its wide base first; a tank fills straight up, a funnel up from its narrow stem, and a pipette its tube and then its cup; the heater's tube holds a shallow stream along its floor. All but 1/e of the gap goes in about 65 s for a full flask, 110 s for a full dispenser, 27 s for a full pipette, and 9 s for a tenth of a flask lying 2 deep across a dispenser's floor. Fluid in mid-air (streams, drops) doesn't cool. Faucets pour at T = 1, so faucet fluid just sits. Besides waiting, the ways to lower a temperature are diluting with faucet fluid, running endothermic reactions, or passing it through the heat exchanger against something cooler. The Chemistry panel's *Cooling* rows edit the rate and the ambient temperature; they aren't saved, and *Restore defaults* puts them back.

### Appearance

- **Color:** by default, a fluid looks like the atom-weighted mix of its colors, and the bond structure is invisible. This is lossy on purpose: R + C averages to grey, for example. (The Appearance panel can try out colors that depend on bonds; see below.)
- **Temperature:** each effect is a smooth function of T, with no cutoff where one effect takes over from another (see `src/game/appearance.ts`).
  - **Cold** fluids fade toward black. The color's brightness is scaled by `1 − e^(−3T)`: black at T = 0 and almost full brightness by T = 1.
  - **Hot** fluids glow. The glow's strength is `ln(1+T) / ln(101)`, and it drives the size and opacity of a corona and a wider halo around the flask. At high T it also bleaches the fluid itself toward white.
  - The glow is subtle but visible at T = 1, obvious at T = 10 and nearly blinding at T = 100.
  - God mode shows the actual number.
  - The **Appearance** debug panel tunes every constant in these curves live. *Copy values* puts the current settings on the clipboard as JSON, and *Restore defaults* undoes your changes.
- **Composition, experimentally:** the Appearance panel can also make color depend on bonds, so reactions show. A fluid is then the atom-weighted average of its molecules' colors, and each molecule's color comes from one of three models:
  - *current*, the default: a molecule is the average of its atoms, so the fluid is the average of its atoms and no reaction changes it;
  - *1: paint*: a molecule's atoms multiply like paints, rescaled to their average's brightness, so C–Y is green and M–Y red;
  - *2: light*: a molecule's atoms add like overlapping spotlights, clipped, so R–G is bright yellow and △RGB white.

  *Mix* blends between the average (0) and the full model (1). The *Cloudiness* checkbox makes bigger molecules more opaque: the fluid's opacity is the atom-weighted average of an α for singles, pairs and triples.

## The puzzle's chemistry

Blue bonds are uphill (E = −1 for now) and have no prefactor (A = 0), so they never form or break on their own, at any temperature. Blue gets into a molecule, or out of one, only by swapping places with yellow, its opposite color. The target, the **△RGB triangle**, sits above R–G + B, so it never dominates an equilibrium.

Yellow bonds have a high barrier (Ea = 10), so at room temperature they hardly form or break, and it takes heat to bond yellow to anything or free it. That keeps a trace of yellow from eating the target. Yellow still swaps in for blue freely (swaps have no barrier), so each yellow atom spoils one triangle into △RGY. But that ring can't open cold, so the yellow stays stuck there instead of moving on to the next triangle. Hot, it does move on, and the damage keeps growing.

The faucets pour single atoms only, so every molecule, the target included, has to be made by mixing. Blue can't be bonded to anything straight from its faucet, since blue bonds never form on their own: it gets in only by swapping with yellow.

And the same from every other spout, nozzle and faucet that would land in, or just miss, something you hold (a flask, a tool's tanks, or the flasks on a held scale). The rim of each mouth a guide ends at is outlined to match, solid or faint: a ring round a round mouth (a flask's, a funnel's), a frame round a tank's. Nothing shows when nothing's held, or for an outlet with no mouth near below it. The real streams, in their fluid's colors, still show over the guides when fluid runs.
- **The grid:** so nothing has to be lined up by eye, everything set down stands on the floor's grid of 100 × 100 cells, facing one of the four ways along it, with every mouth and spout over the middle of a cell. A flask stands with its mouth over a cell's middle; a tool with its first tank's mouth over one, and since every tool's tanks and spouts are a whole number of cells apart along it, all of them are; a scale with both its places for a flask over two (see **Scale**); and a hose's nozzle over one. So a spout over a cell pours straight into whatever mouth is in that cell below it. What you hold moves freely, facing the nearest way along the grid; let go of, it moves the least way that puts it on the grid, into the cell under where you held it, and comes to rest there (see **Controls**), at the height you held it if it's on a stand. Height isn't on a grid. While you hold it, green guides show it as it will be once let go: from each of its spouts (or nozzle) that will then pour into a mouth, and into each of its mouths from every outlet that will then pour into it, with that mouth's rim outlined green. (A flask's lip has none, since a flask pours only while held.) Presets, saves and older saves load onto the grid, everything moved the least way that puts it there; a tool an older save left so near the back wall that its stand is off the counter comes forward a cell or two.
- **Carrying:** whatever you hold catches anything that falls into it, from a faucet, a spout, a drop or a spill, as anything else does. A held tool keeps running, so carrying part of an assembly doesn't interrupt it: what leaves a spout with its hose out still comes out of the hose's nozzle, wherever that is, but what leaves a spout with no hose out goes straight down the drain (drawn falling to the floor), never into anything you carry it over. And picking up a tool doesn't disconnect it: each of its spouts with no hose out that's letting something out (streaming, or gathering a drop) over an open top (but its own) has its hose pulled out as you pick it up, the nozzle left right where the spout was on a stand wherever there's room (the way you face first, as for a hose you pull out), so it keeps pouring there. If one has no room for its stand, the tool stays put and a note says so. Q rolls those hoses back up as it puts the tool back, and so does setting the tool down with a spout right where its hose's nozzle is. Nor does picking something up cut off what pours into it: every faucet, hose's nozzle, or spout letting something out (streaming, or gathering a drop) whose stream lands in what you pick up (a flask, a tool's open tanks, or the flasks on a scale) has its hose, pulled out if it isn't, go along with it, its nozzle kept just where it was over it and turned with it, so it keeps pouring in as you carry it. (An idle spout that happens to be over it is left be.) While carried, those nozzles have no stands and bump into nothing. When you set it down, each is set down over the same spot of it, on the grid, on a stand where there's room: as high over it as it was if there's room, or else as low as there is, from 15 over its mouth up to 300; their stands' bases are outlined with its own. If one can't be, it can't be set down there, and the note says so. Q, or putting it away, puts those hoses back where they were, rolling up any pulled out for it. A hose's nozzle in hand lets nothing out without the right button: the tool or faucet it's pulled out of stops, hanging drops and all, until you press it.

- **Notes:** press **N** on anything that can be carried (a flask, a tool, a scale), or a faucet's knob, to write a note about it: the mouse is let go, and you type up to 24 characters, then Enter or click anywhere else to keep it, or Escape to leave it as it was. On a tool with one tank, the note is that tank's label, just above the tank, above the tool's own label if it has one; on the heat exchanger, N on a tank labels that tank, and anywhere else on it writes a note for the whole exchanger, above both. - **Tools** (`src/game/tools.ts`, and `src/game/bodies.ts` for where their parts are in the room) are glass tanks and pipes, built from their flat outlines on the old bench. Their glass stays nearly clear but reflects the room in full, more so edge on, as real glass does; pipes are stainless, cabinets light powder-coated with rounded edges (as are the scale and the stands' bases), and levers black plastic. Only what's drawn of a tool counts as the tool (its glass included, however faint): the crosshair passes through the empty space around its tanks, pipes and levers to whatever's behind. Each has tanks on top that hold four flasks' worth (the funnels of the splitter, size sorter and heater, and the spectrometer's sample cup, are smaller). Every vessel is drawn just big enough inside to hold what it holds, as much room per atom as a flask's inside (a flask holds a billion in about 63,000 cubic world units), so how much fluid each looks to have agrees from one to the next: each tank is as deep front to back as it needs to be for its outline (about 50 for a four-flask tank, 30 for a funnel, 6 for a pipette's tube), and its fluid settles into it as drawn, filling a funnel's narrow stem, a sump or a pipette's tube first. How fast a tank drains still goes by how high its fluid stands in its flat outline, as before. Each tank drains through its own valve, a round core with a lever on the tool's front face, and fluid leaves through one or more spouts on the bottom. Look at a valve, or at the tool's face within 15 of a valve's center (about the length of its lever), and hold the right button: the mouse stops turning your view and instead moves a little ring across the screen, starting at the crosshair, and the lever points at the ring, as it lies on the tool's front face. Fully open is straight up from the valve, closed straight right, and partly open in between. A valve lets out its openness times the tank's full flow (1 flask/s), times how high the fluid stands as a share of the tank's height: a full tank pours at the full rate and a quarter-full one at a quarter of it. So a tank slows as it drains. Flat-bottomed tanks (the dispenser's, the exchanger's, the separator's and the big tank's) have a sump: a narrow point under the middle of the floor, with the valve at its tip, and the height counts from that tip. A thin layer across the floor still has the sump's depth behind it, so it keeps draining steadily, and once only the narrow point is wet, its level drops fast for how little is left, so the tank empties in a finite time instead of trickling on for ever. A full dispenser fully open is empty in about 14 s. The fluid is drawn filling the sump first. Only within 6 of the valve's center, its round core, does the lever stay put, since the angle there is too jumpy to aim with. Valves start closed. The right button anywhere else on a tool does nothing. Press F looking at a tool, or while holding one, to flip it left to right, about as near its middle as keeps it on the grid: a mirror image, not a turn, since turning it round with Shift would show you its back. Everything about it mirrors (its spouts, tanks, chute or tube) except its valves, whose levers still close to the right and open upward, and the dial, which still turns clockwise; writing stays readable. Only tools that work differently flipped can be flipped: the separator, size sorter and resistive heater. The rest are their own mirror images in what they do, so F does nothing to them. Being flipped is saved. Where valves crowd together, the nearest one turns. A dial (the heater's) turns the same way, pointing at the ring: clockwise from down-left (off) to down-right (full), with straight below going to the nearer end.
  - **Spouts** pour into the first open top straight below them (or below their hose's nozzle, if it's pulled out; see **Hoses**), in a stream whose width goes as the square root of its flow. Below 5M atoms/s (0.005 flask/s of single atoms) an outlet drips instead: what leaves it gathers in a hanging drop, which falls as a Poisson process whose rate, e^((atoms − size) / spread) per second, rises without limit as the drop grows: by default (size 1.5M, spread 0.12M, both set in the Chemistry panel) it's 0.015 per second at 1M atoms, 1 at 1.5M and 64 at 2M, so drops come out a fairly regular size however fast they're fed. Once it lets go, a drop falls under gravity into the first open top below. Hanging drops are saved; drops in mid-air aren't, and neither kind reacts. The open top can be a flask, including one you're holding under the spout, or another tool's tank: anything whose mouth is under the spout, front to back as well as across. If there isn't one, the fluid falls to the counter or the floor and drains away. A stream's width shows everything that left the outlet over the whole frame, so a wide-open valve that drains its tank faster than it's fed still shows its stream at any sim speed.
- **Overflow:** fluid pouring into a full vessel (from a spout, a drop, a faucet, a hose or your own pouring) still goes in and mixes, and the same amount of the mixture spills over the rim: off the right of a flask's lip, or over the right lip of a tank or funnel, as it faces. It falls as a stream, into the first open top below, which may overflow in turn, or down the drain. So a flask of blue under a stream of red turns redder and redder while purple, then red, pours off it. Faucets keep running into a full vessel, too. Chemistry that swells a fluid (by molecules) spills the same way.
  - **Dispenser:** one tank, drained through one spout.
  - **Tank:** a big dispenser: one tank holding a hundred flasks, an upright glass cylinder drawn as big as a hundred flasks are (200 across and 201 tall, in world units where a flask is 70 tall), over a round sump with a spout under it, and its valve out at the front of the tank, on a stem from the pipe under it, so the tank's lower edge doesn't hide it. Ten thin, faint rings round it mark each tenth of what it holds, the last at the brim. Like any tank it pours 1 flask/s full and fully open, in proportion to how high its fluid stands.
  - **Pipette:** a narrow dispenser holding a tenth of a flask, filled through a little funnel on top (whose mouth is what catches streams and drops, and spills over its right lip when full). Its fluid is drawn across tube and funnel together, by area, so it fills the tube first and a full pipette's funnel is full to the brim. Ten graduation marks up its left wall show each tenth of its capacity: evenly up the tube for the first seven, then closer together up the widening funnel, the last at the brim, with the half and the full marks longer. Full and fully open, its valve lets out 0.02 flask/s, a fifth of what it holds each second. Its fluid's height, which sets that flow, runs up its narrow tube and then its cup, so the flow drops off quickly while the cup drains, then more slowly down the tube.
  - **Separator:** one tank, drained through a manifold with five spouts, a grid cell (100) apart so a flask or a tool's tank fits under each. It sorts molecules by their mix of primary-color atoms (R, G, B) and secondary ones (C, M, Y), and each has its own spout, left to right: all primary (R, R–G, △RGB), two in three (△RGY, R–G–Y), half (R–Y), one in three (△RMY), all secondary (C, C–M). By default it's perfect: every molecule goes out its own spout. Below that, a molecule goes to spout j in proportion to e^(−sharpness · (j − its own)²), so at sharpness 3 it goes 91% out its own spout if that's one of the middle three, or 95% at either end, and nearly all the rest out the spouts next to it. The Chemistry panel's *Separator* slider sets the sharpness from 0 (an even five-way split) to 10, the default, which is perfect, showing what share each spout keeps; it isn't saved, and *Restore defaults* puts it back. It splits whole molecules, and heat goes with the atoms. A small handle on one end of the manifold shows which way round it is (it starts on the left), without saying which side gets what.
  - **Heat exchanger:** two tanks, A and B, a grid cell apart, whose streams pass each other in counterflow through two hoses wound into a double helix. Each stream crosses over and leaves from the spout under the *other* tank, and the hoses show each stream's color. They trade heat but never mix, and heat is conserved. It uses the standard effectiveness–NTU model with a fixed exchange capacity (`EXCHANGE_RATE`, 2 flasks/s): the slower stream gets a fraction ε of the way to the other's inlet temperature. Two equal streams at 0.25 flask/s (valves at a quarter, tanks full) nearly swap temperatures (ε = 8/9). At 1 flask/s they get ε = 2/3. If one valve is shut, the other stream passes through unchanged.
  - **Splitter:** a quarter-flask funnel that drains straight through (2 flasks/s when full, and in proportion to how high its fluid stands, like a valve) into a fork with two spouts, a grid cell either side of it. Its valve doesn't open or close: it sets the split. Point the lever left to send everything left, right for everything right, and anywhere in between for a share in between, straight up being even. Below the valve it goes to the nearer side. It starts even, and anything arriving faster than it drains overflows.
  - **Size sorter:** a quarter-flask funnel with no valve that drains (2 flasks/s when full, and in proportion to how high its fluid stands) down a sloping chute, like the screens of a gravel sorter. Each of three spouts, a grid cell apart (the first a cell on from the funnel), sits under one hole, in order down the chute. The chute looks plain all the way down, so the player has to find out what each hole takes. By default it's perfect: singles all fall through the first screen, pairs the second, and triples go off the end of the chute. Weaker, it leaks: each size goes 1/3 + 2/3 · strength out its own spout and the rest evenly out the other two. At strength 0.55, 70% of each size goes out its own spout and 15% out each of the others. The Chemistry panel's *Size sorter* slider sets the strength from 0 (every size splits evenly three ways) to 1 (perfect), showing what share each size keeps; it isn't saved, and *Restore defaults* puts it back. It splits whole molecules, and heat goes with the atoms. The film on the chute shows what's still sliding down.
  - **Resistive heater:** a long horizontal glass tube with a copper wire coiled down its middle. A quarter-flask funnel over its left end drains into it at 1 flask/s when full, and in proportion to how high its fluid stands (anything arriving faster than a full funnel drains overflows), and the tube carries whatever is in it to the right end in 6 s, however much there is, and down a spout there. Three taps along its underside, at the ends of the tube's first three quarters, each a valve over a spout, a grid cell apart, with the end's spout a cell further on, let fluid out sooner: a tap's valve lets out up to 1 flask/s of what's passing, so a wide-open one takes everything and none goes further. A dial on a box above the tube's right end sets the wire's temperature: off at 0, then rising geometrically from room temperature (T = 1) to 100 at full, so a quarter turn is about 3 and halfway 10. The wire goes from copper through glowing red, orange and yellow to white-hot as the dial turns up. Fluid in the tube closes 1 − e^(−0.25 t) of the gap to the wire's temperature in t seconds, so by the taps it's 31%, 53% and 68% of the way, and 78% by the end (less what the tube loses to the room meanwhile, see **Cooling**), though a little spreads out ahead and behind as it goes (the tube is simulated as 24 well-mixed stretches). The wire only heats, so fluid hotter than it passes through unchanged, and with the dial off nothing is heated. Reactions go on in the tube as anywhere else. What's in each stretch is drawn lying along the tube's floor, as deep as that stretch holds when the funnel feeds it flat out. Its feed rate, transit time, heating rate and top wire temperature are editable in the Chemistry panel's *Resistive heater* rows; like the drip settings, they aren't saved, and *Restore defaults* puts them back.

The error is fixed per shelf slot, so weighing fluid means taring with its flask first.
- **Supply and goal:** the cryostabilizer reference (see **Cryostabilizer station**) starts with a hundred flasks of the target, 300 billion atoms, and the intro's 28.10 L left is that, so a flask (a billion molecules) holds about 281 mL. The game is won when the reference holds 50.22 L of the target, the intro's sum (32,210 passengers × 0.0003499 mL a day × 4,456 days): 178.7 flasks, so 78.7 more flasks of it, put in by way of the receptacle and the tester. Only the target in the reference counts, not whatever else got past the tester with it. The reference holds 540 flasks, about three times the goal, so the gauge's red runs up to about a third of the way up. There's no progress bar; the station's screen shows how far along it is. What's in the reference is saved with the lab, and a preset or Reset fills it again with the hundred flasks it starts with. The intro's numbers come from the same constants as the goal (`VOYAGE` in `src/game/config.ts`), so they stay in step.
- **God mode:** the checkbox in the header, off until it's first turned on. Whether it's on is kept in local storage beside the save (not in it, so exporting a lab doesn't carry it), so a refresh leaves it as it was. Looking at a flask or a tank, or holding a flask, shows by the crosshair its temperature, fill level, a species pie chart and the top species. The pie keeps a fixed order (singles, then pairs, then triples, counterclockwise from north), so its sectors don't jump around as amounts shift. The list is sorted by amount. Double-clicking one opens an editor for its temperature and composition, which lists every species in it, down to a single molecule, with its exact count. You can drag or type each number, add or remove any of the 50 species, or empty it. Its Equilibrate button brings the contents to full chemical equilibrium at their temperature at once, with every atom kept, using the same exact solver as the presets. That runs every reaction to its end, including ones the kinetics never get to, like blue bonds forming. If the result is more molecules than fit, the excess spills over the rim. Its Reactions button shows or hides a live list of what's reacting in it, busiest first: each reaction is netted against its reverse and shown the way it's going on net, with its rate in events per sim second. The top 15 are listed, with a count of the rest. The list stays shown or hidden from one opening of the editor to the next. It updates live while the contents react. Outside god mode, looking at a flask or tank shows only a blotch of its fluid's color, the same color it's drawn in, or a dashed outline if it's empty. Outside god mode, the Chemistry and Appearance panels are hidden, and the Sound panel has no spectrometer slider, since the name gives the machine away.
- **Sim speed:** pause, 1×, 4× or 16×, since Arrhenius waiting is boring.
- **Sound** (all synthesized with Web Audio, no audio files; the mixer is `src/game/audio.ts`). Browsers keep audio off until a click or key press, so it starts on the first one.
  - **Ship's ambience** (`src/game/ambience.ts`): from the moment the intro's console log fades (so through the wake-up notice) and on through the game, a warm low hum (partials of 48 Hz, beating slowly against near-twins, over low-passed brown noise, swelling gently), with a faint klaxon whooping (280 to 760 Hz over 1.3 s, every 2.2 s) and a faint fire alarm blaring in the temporal-three pattern (three half-second 520 Hz blares, then a gap, every 4 s), both muffled and echoing as if from down a corridor. It's atmosphere, not the lab, so unlike everything else it plays on in real time, paused or not. It's silent on the intro's start screen and during the log, including on a replay, and fades in over a couple of seconds.
  - **Drips** (`src/game/water.ts`): each drop that lands in a vessel plinks, a short tone around 700–1200 Hz that leaps upward as it dies. Drops that land on a counter or the floor are silent. At most one plinks every 0.07 s, so a crowd of drops at 16× doesn't turn into a buzz.
  - **Streams:** every vessel with a stream running into it (from a spout, a hose, a faucet, your own pouring, or an overflow spilling from above) trickles: band-passed noise that burbles unevenly, with little bubbles popping. It's as loud as the square root of the flow, full at a flask per sim second, and it rings at 240 / (1 − 0.9 · fullness) Hz, so it rises tenfold as the vessel fills, like a bottle under a tap. The six loudest streams are heard. Flow is measured per sim second, so sim speed doesn't change the sound, and pausing silences it.
  - **Spectrometer:** its rumble and chimes (see above).
  - **Cryostabilizer station:** its blips, verdict tones and flushes (see above; `src/game/beeper.ts`).
  - **Where sounds come from** (`src/game/place.ts`): drips, streams, the spectrometer and the cryostabilizer station (from its panel) are heard from where they are relative to your head. Within 400 they play at full volume; further off they halve with every doubling of the distance, fading to silence at 6000. They pan toward the ear they're on, up to 0.8 of the way for something straight off to one side, and something straight behind you is 0.6 as loud as the same thing ahead. The ship's ambience comes from everywhere.
  - **Volume:** the *Sound* panel has a slider for each of these (hum, klaxon, fire alarm, drips, streams, spectrometer, receptacle) and one for everything. Gain goes as the square of the slider, so 50 plays a sound as synthesized, the top four times as loud, and the bottom silent. They start at 50, except the far-off klaxon at 23 and fire alarm at 26, and *Restore defaults* puts them back there. Sliders are kept in local storage, not in saves.
- **Presets:** the dropdown next to Reset loads a starting layout, and Reset restarts the current one. *Stranded* is the game. *Temperature range* shows flasks from T = 0 to T = 100, to show how temperature looks. *Heat exchanger demo* passes hot red and room-temperature green through the exchanger. *Separator demo* sorts red from cyan, into dispensers under the two end spouts. *Wash route (sandbox)* is a sandbox to tinker with: a hot separator of △RGY and blue with its two left spouts (all primary, and two in three) hosed back into its tank, hot blue dripping in from above, and a catch tank under the far right spout (all secondary). Picking it from the menu also restores the default chemistry. Presets are defined in `src/game/presets.ts`, and can pull out hoses, naming the spout each comes from and the tank its nozzle goes over. The dropdown shows only in god mode; outside it, Reset still restarts the current preset.
- **Chemistry table:** every parameter is live-editable. Drag a number sideways to scale it by 1% per pixel (100 px ≈ ×e). Double-click a bond energy `E` to flip its sign, or any other number to type it, which is how to set an `A` back to 0. *Restore defaults* puts the default chemistry back, volume back to counting molecules, and the drip, heater, cooling, separator, size sorter and flow meter settings back too. All reaction rates rebuild on every change.
- **Saving** (`src/game/save.ts`): the lab (every flask, tool, scale and hose, where each is and which way it faces (and how far a hose's stand is behind its nozzle), their contents (a heater's tube included), valves, tares and notes), the sticky notes, where you stand and look, and the chemistry parameters are saved to local storage every 2 seconds and when you leave the page, and restored on load. Positions across and up are stored as on the old flat bench, with how far out from the back wall and which way things face added as optional fields, so older saves still load; anything outside the room is brought inside it. *Export* copies the whole setup as a string (base64 of JSON), and *Import* loads one. Contents are stored by species name, so saves survive reordering the species list.

## Open questions / next steps

- **Separation.** The separator and size sorter split species only partly. Candidates for more:
  - boiling: small species are volatile, which reuses temperature;
  - a size sieve: singles pass, pairs and triples stay;
  - a color trap: an item that binds one free color.

  The choice affects what shape the target should be.
- **Temperature control.** The resistive heater heats, and everything cools to the room. Nothing chills below room temperature yet except endotherms; an ice bath would. The *Temperature range* preset's flasks now drift to room temperature over a minute or two.
- **More glassware and tools.** Graduated cylinders and pipettes.
- **Tool follow-ups.**
  - A tank can only be emptied through its spout.
  - Tools can't stand on the scale, so a dispenser's contents can't be weighed.
  - Every atom weighs the same. Giving colors different masses would make the scale reveal something about composition.
- **Tuning.**
  - The swap prefactor: if it's too high, recoloring is trivial and the whole puzzle is getting the topology right.
  - Heat capacity: R + G alone heats up noticeably.
  - Whether the six colors stay distinguishable in mixtures.
- **Playtester notes** (not yet acted on):
  - Make sure there's a synthesis pathway: a route that actually reaches more than 95% cryostabilizer.
  - Maybe add an off switch to the new device. The note doesn't say which device is meant.
  - Maybe add a mixer.
  - Add some sort of UI tutorial on how to change valve positions (look at a valve, hold the right button and point).

## Development

```sh
npm install
npm run dev        # local dev server
npm test           # chemistry tests (vitest)
npm run route      # a synthesis harness, printed stage by stage (src/game/route.test.ts)
npm run build      # typecheck + production build into dist/
```

Code layout:

- `src/chem/`: the chemistry model, pure TypeScript with no DOM. It covers atoms, species enumeration, parameters, and the reaction network with its integrator.
- `src/game/`: flasks, tools, the scale, pouring, fluid color, and `GameEngine`, which owns the lab: the player, input, collisions (`physics.ts`) and the simulation loop. `view.ts` draws it with Three.js, and `bodies.ts` says where each thing's parts are in the room. `route.ts` is a harness that runs a synthesis on the same pieces without the UI and reports each stage.
- `src/components/`: the React UI around the canvas: speed control, the hotbar, the crosshair's hints, the chemistry table and the god-mode panel.
